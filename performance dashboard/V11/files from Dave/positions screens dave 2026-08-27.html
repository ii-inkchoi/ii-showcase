Track Record & Why 5 Years

A short track record can't separate skill from luck — a good year or two is easily variance, not edge. Five years is roughly the point a return series starts to say something statistically meaningful about an active manager versus an index, because it's long enough to include at least one full market cycle. The bar tracks distance to that mark, not to a performance goal — it fills the same amount whether the account is up or down.

Performance vs S&P

Three numbers, always shown together rather than as one pre-computed figure: what the account actually returned in dollars and percent, what the same money would have returned sitting in the S&P 500 over the identical window (adjusted for your actual deposit and withdrawal timing, not a static index price return), and Alpha — the difference between the two. Alpha is the honest answer to "did active management add value, or would I have done just as well in an index fund." A positive number means yes; negative means the S&P would have done better with the same money and timing.

Cash Drag

The return given up by holding uninvested cash instead of being fully deployed. Cash earns close to nothing while it sits idle; if the market rallies during that time, holding it quietly costs real return even though nothing went wrong with any position. This figure is what that idle cash would have earned had it tracked the S&P 500 instead — a hidden cost that never shows up in any individual position's numbers, only at the account level.

Time-Weighted Return (TWR)

The compound growth rate of $1 invested, with the effect of your deposits and withdrawals mathematically removed. It's built by splitting the period into sub-periods around each cash flow, calculating the return for each slice separately, then chaining them together. Because timing is stripped out, TWR isolates pure investment selection — it answers "how good were the picks," independent of when money moved in or out. This is the standard figure used to compare a strategy or manager against a benchmark, which is why it's the number most often quoted as "the return."

Money-Weighted Return (MWR)

Also called the internal rate of return. Unlike TWR, this does account for the size and timing of deposits and withdrawals — a large deposit made right before a rally gets more credit under MWR than under TWR; a deposit made right before a downturn gets penalized more. MWR answers a different question than TWR: not "how good were the picks," but "how did my actual dollars actually do," including the consequences of when I happened to add or remove money.

TWR − MWR Gap (Timing Skill)

This is the number that actually matters most, and it's easy to miss if you only look at TWR or MWR individually. The gap isolates the impact of cash-flow timing, separate from stock selection. If MWR is lower than TWR, it usually means deposits landed before weaker periods, or withdrawals happened before stronger ones — poor timing dragged down the dollar-weighted result even if the underlying picks (TWR) were fine. If MWR is higher, timing helped. Two people who owned the identical portfolio can have meaningfully different real outcomes purely from when they moved money — TWR can't see that difference, MWR can, and this gap is the two compared directly.

Max Drawdown

The largest peak-to-trough decline in the account's value over the period, expressed as a percent. This is a pain measure, not a return measure — it captures how bad the worst moment was, regardless of how the period ended overall. Two accounts finishing at the identical return can have very different max drawdowns; the one with the deeper drawdown was a rockier ride that demanded more discipline to stay invested through. The S&P figure shown alongside it is the index's own worst decline over that same window, for direct comparison — was this account's roughest patch worse than the market's, or better cushioned than it?

Volatility (12M)

The standard deviation of monthly returns over the trailing 12 months, annualized — a measure of how much the account's value swings, in either direction, not a measure of direction itself. Higher volatility means bigger swings up and down; it's a proxy for risk and uncertainty, not a verdict on performance. A high-volatility account isn't necessarily worse than a low-volatility one — some strategies deliberately take on more swing in exchange for higher expected return — which is why this row stays neutral even when it's above the S&P's own figure.

Avg & Median Return, Profitable / Loss

Every stat above this point is bucketed by beat/trailed S&P — this group is bucketed differently, by whether the position simply made or lost money, in raw return terms with no benchmark involved at all. Average can be pulled around by one outsized position; median shows the typical, middle-of-the-pack result, which is why both are shown together rather than just one.

Reading this next to the Alpha numbers above it is the point — a position can be a big raw winner and a middling alpha performer at the same time, or the reverse, and neither set of numbers can tell you that on its own.

What the S&P 500 returned over the exact same window this position was held — same entry date, same exit date. Shown as a real number, not a hidden delta, so it can be checked directly against your own return.

Color

Color always means beat or trailed the index — never just profit or loss. Tap into any position for the full dollar breakdown: cost basis, raw gain, and dollar alpha.

Profitable ≠ Beat the Benchmark

A position can make money and still trail the S&P — the subhead's "Profitable / Unprofitable" figure and the "Beat S&P / Trailed" filter answer two different questions, and they don't have to agree. Of the 9 closed positions here, 7 were profitable, but only 5 actually beat the index.

Held Days & Closed Date

How long the position was open, and when it was closed. Two positions can post the same final return over very different holding periods — a return earned in weeks and one that took years aren't the same outcome, even at an identical percent.

Filter Chips (Beat S&P / Trailed)

Splits the list by final result — Beat S&P means the position outperformed the index over its full holding window, Trailed means it underperformed. Counts here always match the rows actually rendered under each filter.

Win Rate

The percentage of closed positions that beat the S&P — 55.6% here. This is about frequency, not size: it doesn't say whether the wins were large or the losses were small, only how often the outcome favored beating the index.

Sort (Date Closed)

Orders the list by when each position was exited, most recent first by default — useful for reviewing recent decisions before older ones.

Alpha is the one number on this screen shown standalone — safe to trust, because its two inputs (Position, S&P 500) sit directly above it.
Portfolio Impact tells you if this decision actually mattered to your results, not just whether it "won" in isolation.
Status Pill

Open positions read "Beating S&P" or "Trailing S&P" — present tense, since the outcome isn't final. Closed positions read "Beat S&P" or "Trailed" — past tense, since it's settled. This is the same beat/trail judgment used everywhere else, at the single-position level.

Total Gain (Hero)

The dollar amount this position actually gained or lost, from entry to exit (or entry to today, if still open) — cost basis times return percent. This is the plain-dollar answer to "did I make money," before any comparison to the benchmark.

Your Return / S&P Return (Hero Sub)

The same two-number comparison used on the list screens, at full resolution: your percent return next to the S&P's percent return over the identical window. Neither number is hidden behind the other.

Entered / Closed / Held

The exact dates the position was opened and closed, and the number of days between them. Holding period gives context to the return — the same percent gain means something different earned in three months versus three years.

Entry Price / Exit Price

The per-share price paid at entry and received at exit (or the current price, if still open). This is the raw input the return percent is calculated from — useful for sanity-checking the math yourself, or for comparing against the price you remember paying.

Entry Weight

What percent of total portfolio value this position represented when it was first purchased — a measure of conviction and concentration risk. A position sized at 2% of the account matters far less to overall performance than one sized at 20%, even if both post identical percent returns. Weight is what turns a percent return into an actual impact on the account.

Three rows, and the one place in this feature where Alpha is shown as its own number — because the two figures it's built from sit directly above it, always visible, so it's never a number you have to trust blindly.

Alpha — the difference between the two, in both dollars and percentage points. Alpha here is derived, not asserted — it always equals Position minus S&P, and you can check that yourself from the two rows above it.

Position vs Benchmark Chart

The position's value plotted against the S&P 500 over the same holding window, both starting from the same point so the lines are directly comparable. Where the solid line pulls away from the dashed line is where alpha was actually earned or lost — useful for seeing whether outperformance happened steadily or in one sharp move.

Portfolio Impact

How much this single position mattered to the whole account, not just how it did in isolation.

Dollar contribution to account return
The actual dollars this position added to (or subtracted from) total account performance.
Share of total return this period
What percent of the account's entire gain for the period this one position was responsible for — a position can be a big winner in isolation and still be a small share of the total account story if it was lightly weighted.
Rank among closed positions
Where this position ranks by dollar contribution against every other closed position — answers "how big a deal was this decision," not just "was it a good one."
